
# Governance and Regulation in Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity threats are continuously changing. Attackers try to find vulnerabilities in systems to:

* Steal sensitive company data.
* Damage systems.
* Disrupt business operations.
* Gain unauthorized access.

Because of these threats, organizations cannot depend only on tools such as firewalls, antivirus, and IDS.

They also need **rules, policies, standards, risk management, and legal compliance**.

This is where **Governance and Regulation** become important.

## Important Terminologies

| Term           | Simple Meaning                                                                                  |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Governance** | Managing and directing an organization to achieve its goals while following laws and standards. |
| **Regulation** | A rule or law enforced by a government or regulatory body.                                      |
| **Compliance** | Following the laws, regulations, and standards that apply to an organization.                   |

### Simple Example

Imagine a company stores customer data.

**Governance:** The company decides how customer data should be protected.

**Regulation:** A law says the company must protect personal data.

**Compliance:** The company follows that law and proves that the required security controls are implemented.

So, simply remember:

**Governance = Manage and Direct**

**Regulation = Rules and Laws**

**Compliance = Follow the Rules**

# Information Security Governance

**Information Security Governance** is the structure, policies, processes, and guidelines an organization uses to protect its information.

Its main goal is to maintain:

* **Confidentiality** – Only authorized people can access information.
* **Integrity** – Information should not be changed without authorization.
* **Availability** – Information and systems should be available when required.

These three principles are known as the **CIA Triad**.

Information security governance is mainly the responsibility of **senior management and organizational leadership**.

It helps the organization:

* Manage cybersecurity risks.
* Protect sensitive information.
* Follow laws and regulations.
* Align security with business goals.

## Information Security Governance Processes

### 1. Strategy

The organization creates an information security strategy that supports its business goals.

For example, if a company provides online banking, its security strategy should strongly focus on protecting customer accounts and financial transactions.

### 2. Policies and Procedures

The organization creates rules explaining how systems and information should be used and protected.

Examples include:

* Password Policy
* Access Control Policy
* Data Protection Policy
* Incident Response Policy

A **policy** defines what must be done.

A **procedure** explains how to do it.

### 3. Risk Management

The organization identifies possible threats and vulnerabilities.

It then evaluates the risk and applies security controls to reduce it.

Simple flow:

**Identify Risk → Analyze Risk → Reduce Risk → Monitor Risk**

For example, a company identifies phishing as a major threat. It may implement email filtering and employee security awareness training to reduce the risk.

### 4. Performance Measurement

Organizations must check whether their security program is actually working.

They use **metrics** and **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)**.

Examples:

* Number of security incidents.
* Time required to detect an attack.
* Time required to respond to an incident.
* Number of employees completing security training.

KPIs help management measure security performance.

### 5. Compliance

The organization ensures that it follows applicable laws, regulations, and industry standards.

Examples include:

* GDPR
* HIPAA
* PCI DSS

Failure to comply may result in fines, legal problems, or reputational damage.


# Information Security Regulation

**Information Security Regulation** refers to laws and regulatory requirements that control how organizations protect and use information.

The main goal is to protect sensitive information from:

* Unauthorized access.
* Theft.
* Misuse.
* Data breaches.

Unlike internal company policies, compliance with applicable laws and regulations is often **mandatory**.

Government agencies or regulatory bodies may enforce these requirements.

### Simple Difference

| Governance                    | Regulation                                                      |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Managed by the organization.  | Created or enforced by external authorities or industry bodies. |
| Guides security decisions.    | Defines legal or mandatory requirements.                        |
| Focuses on managing security. | Focuses on required rules and protections.                      |


# Benefits of Governance and Regulation

**More Robust Security Posture:** A security posture means the organization's overall level of cybersecurity protection. Good governance helps reduce security risks and protect sensitive information.

**Increased Stakeholder Confidence:** Stakeholders include customers, employees, investors, and business partners. Strong security practices increase their trust in the organization.

**Regulatory Compliance:** Following regulations helps organizations avoid legal penalties, financial fines, and reputational damage.

**Alignment with Business Objectives:** Security should support the organization's business goals. Governance ensures that security investments are useful and cost-effective.

**Informed Decision-Making:** Security governance provides management with information about threats and risks. This helps leaders decide where security controls are most needed.

**Competitive Advantage:** Organizations with strong security and compliance can gain more customer trust and may have an advantage over competitors.

# Information Security Frameworks

An **Information Security Framework** is a structured collection of documents that explains **how an organization should manage, implement, and enforce cybersecurity**.

Think of it as the organization's **security rulebook and instruction manual**.

It mainly contains **Policies, Standards, Guidelines, Procedures, and Baselines**.

## Important Security Documents

| Document      | Simple Meaning                                 | Mandatory? |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| **Policy**    | High-level security rule or goal               | Yes        |
| **Standard**  | Specific requirement that must be followed     | Yes        |
| **Guideline** | Recommended best practice                      | No         |
| **Procedure** | Step-by-step instructions to perform a task    | Yes        |
| **Baseline**  | Minimum security level that must be maintained | Yes        |

### Policy

A **Policy** is a formal statement that defines the organization's security goals and rules.

For example, a password policy may state:

**Employees must use strong passwords.**

The policy tells us **what must be done**.

### Standard

A **Standard** defines specific requirements.

For example:

**Passwords must contain at least 12 characters.**

**Passwords must include uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and special characters.**

A standard gives **exact requirements**.

### Guideline

A **Guideline** provides recommendations and best practices.

For example:

**Consider using a password manager to create and store passwords.**

Guidelines are usually **not mandatory**.

They tell us **what is recommended**.

### Procedure

A **Procedure** provides exact step-by-step instructions for completing a task.

For example, a password reset procedure may be:

1. Verify the user's identity.
2. Open the user management system.
3. Select the user account.
4. Generate a temporary password.
5. Require the user to change the password after login.

A procedure explains **how to perform a task**.

### Baseline

A **Baseline** defines the minimum acceptable security requirements.

For example:

All company computers must have:

* Antivirus enabled.
* Firewall enabled.
* Disk encryption enabled.
* Latest security updates installed.

If a system does not meet these requirements, it is **below the organization's security baseline**.

### Easy Way to Remember

**Policy = What must we do?**

**Standard = What exact requirement must we follow?**

**Guideline = What is recommended?**

**Procedure = How do we do it?**

**Baseline = What is the minimum security level?**


# Developing Governance Documents

Organizations follow a general process when creating security policies, standards, procedures, guidelines, or baselines.

### 1. Identify Scope and Purpose

First, determine:

* What will the document cover?
* Why is the document required?
* Who does the document apply to?

For example, a **Password Policy** may be required to ensure that employees use secure passwords.

A **Security Baseline** may be required to define minimum security requirements for all company computers.

### 2. Research and Review

The organization researches:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Industry standards.
* Security best practices.
* Existing company documents.

This ensures that the new document is correct, updated, and does not conflict with existing policies.

### 3. Draft the Document

The organization creates the first version of the document.

The document should be:

* Clear.
* Simple.
* Specific.
* Actionable (possible to follow).
* Aligned with business goals.

### 4. Review and Approval

The document is reviewed by relevant stakeholders.

**Stakeholders** are people or teams affected by or responsible for the document.

Examples include:

* Cybersecurity team.
* IT team.
* Legal team.
* Compliance team.
* Senior management.

They review the document, provide feedback, and approve the final version.

### 5. Implementation and Communication

After approval, the organization introduces the document.

Employees must be informed about the new rules and their responsibilities.

The organization may provide:

* Security training.
* Awareness programs.
* Emails.
* Internal documentation.

Creating a policy is not enough. **Employees must understand and follow it.**

### 6. Review and Update

Security documents should not remain unchanged forever.

Organizations regularly review them because:

* New cyber threats appear.
* Technologies change.
* Business requirements change.
* Laws and regulations change.

The organization monitors compliance and updates the document when necessary.

The complete process is:

**Identify → Research → Draft → Review and Approve → Implement → Review and Update**

# Real-World Example: Password Policy

* **Define password requirements:**

  * Set minimum password length.
  * Define password complexity (uppercase, lowercase, numbers, symbols).
  * Define password expiration time.

* **Define password usage guidelines:**

  * Use a unique password for each account.
  * Do not share passwords with others.
  * Do not use default passwords.

* **Define password storage and transmission guidelines:**

  * Use encryption to store passwords securely.
  * Use secure connections to transmit passwords.

* **Define password change and reset guidelines:**

  * Define how often passwords should be changed.
  * Define a secure password reset process.

* **Communicate the policy:**

  * Inform employees and stakeholders about the password policy.
  * Make sure they understand the requirements and guidelines.
  * Provide training and awareness programs.
  * Ensure employees follow the policy.

* **Monitor compliance:**

  * Check whether employees follow the password policy.
  * Update the policy based on feedback.
  * Adjust the policy when threats or regulations change.



# Real-World Example: Incident Response Procedure

* **Define incident types:**

  * Identify different types of incidents.
  * Examples: Unauthorised access, malware infections, and data breaches.

* **Define incident response roles and responsibilities:**

  * Identify the stakeholders involved.
  * Assign responsibilities to the incident response team.
  * Include IT personnel, legal and compliance teams, and senior management.

* **Detailed Steps:**

  * Create step-by-step procedures for each type of incident.
  * **Initial response:** Contain the incident and preserve evidence.
  * **Analysis and investigation:** Identify the root cause and assess the impact.
  * **Response and recovery:** Mitigate the incident, report it, and restore normal operations.

* **Report the incident to management and document the incident response process for future reference.**

  * Report the incident to management.
  * Document the response process for future reference.

* **Communicate the incident response procedures.**

  * Inform employees about the incident response procedures.
  * Ensure everyone understands their responsibilities.

* **Review and update the incident response procedures.**

  * Regularly review the procedures.
  * Update them based on lessons learned, new threats, or policy changes.

# Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC)

We already learned that **governance and compliance are important for maintaining an organization's security posture**.

The question is: **How does an organization manage governance, security risks, and compliance together?**

This is where **GRC** is used.

**GRC stands for Governance, Risk Management, and Compliance.**

GRC is an integrated approach that helps an organization:

* Manage cybersecurity.
* Identify and reduce risks.
* Follow laws and industry standards.
* Align security with business goals.

Simple meaning:

**GRC = Manage the organization + Manage risks + Follow requirements**

# Three Components of GRC

| Component           | Simple Meaning                                                  |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Governance**      | Decide the security direction and rules.                        |
| **Risk Management** | Find risks and reduce them.                                     |
| **Compliance**      | Follow laws, regulations, standards, and internal requirements. |

### Governance

The **Governance component** guides the organization's security direction.

The organization creates:

* Security strategies.
* Policies.
* Standards.
* Baselines.
* Frameworks.

Governance also defines methods to **monitor security performance and measure results**.

For example, management may create a password policy and then monitor whether employees and systems follow the policy.

Simply remember:

**Governance = Decide how security should be managed.**

### Risk Management

The **Risk Management component** focuses on identifying and managing cybersecurity risks.

The basic process is:

**Identify Risk → Assess Risk → Prioritize Risk → Apply Controls → Monitor Risk**

For example, an organization identifies **phishing attacks** as a major risk.

Possible controls include:

* Email filtering.
* Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).
* Employee security training.

The organization continuously monitors the risk and improves its security controls when necessary.

Simply remember:

**Risk Management = Find the danger and reduce its impact.**

### Compliance

The **Compliance component** ensures that the organization follows applicable:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Industry standards.
* Internal policies and procedures.

Compliance activities include:

* Creating compliance programs.
* Performing security audits.
* Conducting assessments.
* Reporting compliance problems to stakeholders.

For example, a company handling payment card information may need to comply with **PCI DSS**.

Simply remember:

**Compliance = Check whether the organization is following the required rules.**

# How to Develop a GRC Program

Developing a GRC program involves several steps.

The complete flow is:

**Define → Assess → Develop → Govern → Control → Monitor → Improve**

### 1. Define Scope and Objectives

First, decide **where the GRC program will be applied and what it should achieve**.

**Scope** means the area covered by the program.

For example, a company may implement a GRC program for its **customer data management system**.

An objective may be to reduce cybersecurity risks by **50% within 12 months** while maintaining customer trust.

### 2. Conduct a Risk Assessment

The organization identifies and analyzes possible cybersecurity risks.

For example, the customer data system may have:

* Weak access controls.
* Outdated software.
* Vulnerable applications.

The organization assesses these risks and decides which risks should be handled first.

High-risk problems receive higher priority.

### 3. Develop Policies and Procedures

The organization creates security policies and procedures.

For example:

A **Password Policy** may require employees to use strong passwords.

A **Logging and Monitoring Procedure** may explain how security teams should monitor system access and identify suspicious activity.

Remember:

**Policy = What must be done**

**Procedure = How to do it**

### 4. Establish Governance Processes

The organization creates a structure to manage the GRC program.

For example, it may create a **Security Steering Committee**.

The committee regularly:

* Reviews security risks.
* Discusses security problems.
* Decides security priorities.
* Approves security investments.

Roles and responsibilities are also defined so everyone knows what they are responsible for.

### 5. Implement Security Controls

The organization implements controls to reduce identified risks.

A **Security Control** is a safeguard used to reduce risk.

Technical controls may include:

* Firewalls.
* Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS).
* Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS).
* Security Information and Event Management (SIEM).

Non-technical controls may include:

* Employee security training.
* Security awareness programs.
* Policies and procedures.

For example, a firewall may reduce network attacks, while employee training may reduce phishing risks.

### 6. Monitor and Measure Performance

The organization checks whether the GRC program is working effectively.

It can monitor:

* Number of security incidents.
* Policy compliance.
* Detected threats.
* Incident response time.
* Employee training completion.

These measurements help identify weaknesses in the GRC program.

### 7. Continuously Improve

A GRC program must be regularly reviewed and improved.

Cyber threats, technologies, and regulations continuously change.

For example, if a security incident occurs, the organization performs a **post-incident analysis**.

The organization asks:

* What happened?
* Why did it happen?
* What was the root cause?
* Which security control failed?
* How can we prevent it from happening again?

The organization then improves its security controls and processes.

# GRC Example in the Financial Sector

Banks and financial companies handle sensitive customer and financial information.

Because of this, they need strong GRC programs.

### Governance Activities

The financial organization appoints senior executives responsible for governance.

It also creates financial and security-related policies, such as:

* Anti-Money Laundering (AML) policies.
* Financial audit policies.
* Financial reporting policies.
* Crisis management policies.

The goal is to define **how the organization should operate and manage security**.

### Risk Management Activities

The organization identifies financial and cybersecurity risks.

Examples include:

* Financial fraud.
* Fraudulent online transactions.
* Credentials stolen through phishing.
* Fake ATM cards.
* Cyberattacks against banking systems.

The organization evaluates each risk and implements security controls to reduce it.

### Compliance Activities

Financial organizations must follow relevant legal and industry requirements.

Examples include:

* PCI DSS.
* GLBA.

The organization may also implement security measures such as:

* SSL/TLS to protect network communication.
* Automatic patch management to fix vulnerable software.
* Security awareness campaigns to reduce phishing attacks.

These activities help the organization meet its compliance and security requirements.

# Easy GRC Example

Imagine a bank is experiencing phishing attacks.

**Governance:** The bank creates an email security policy and assigns a security team to manage phishing risks.

**Risk Management:** The bank identifies phishing as a high risk and implements email filtering, MFA, and employee training.

**Compliance:** The bank performs audits and checks whether required policies, laws, and standards are being followed.

This is how **Governance + Risk Management + Compliance work together**.

# Privacy and Data Protection Regulations

Organizations in sectors such as **finance, healthcare, government, and industry** handle sensitive personal information.

This information is often called **Personally Identifiable Information (PII)**.

**PII** is information that can be used to identify a person directly or indirectly.

Examples include:

* Full name
* Email address
* Phone number
* Government ID number
* Home address
* Financial information

Privacy and data protection regulations define **how organizations should collect, use, store, process, and protect personal information**.

Their main goals are to protect personal data, prevent misuse, build trust, and ensure regulatory compliance.


# General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

The **GDPR** is a data protection law introduced by the **European Union (EU)** in **May 2018**.

Its main purpose is to protect the personal data of individuals in the EU.

**Personal Data** means any information that can directly or indirectly identify a person.

For example, a person's name can directly identify them, while information such as an IP address or other combined data may indirectly identify them.

## Important GDPR Requirements

### 1. Obtain Approval Before Collecting Data

Organizations should have a valid legal basis for collecting and processing personal data. In situations where they rely on **consent**, that consent must be obtained properly before processing.

For example, a website may ask a user for consent before using certain optional tracking cookies.

### 2. Collect Only Necessary Data

Organizations should collect only the personal data required for a specific purpose.

For example, if a website only needs your email address to send a newsletter, it should not unnecessarily ask for passport information.

This idea is called **Data Minimization**.

Simply remember:

**Collect only the data you actually need.**

### 3. Protect Stored Personal Data

Organizations must implement suitable security measures to protect personal information.

Examples include:

* Encryption.
* Access control.
* Strong authentication.
* Security monitoring.
* Secure data storage.

The goal is to prevent unauthorized access, theft, loss, or misuse of personal data.


# Who Must Follow GDPR?

GDPR can apply to organizations that process the personal data of individuals in the **European Union**, including some organizations located outside the EU when their activities fall within GDPR's territorial scope.

For example, a company outside Europe may still have GDPR obligations if it offers goods or services to people in the EU or monitors their behavior there.

The organization must have a lawful reason for processing personal data and must provide people with information about how their data is used.


# GDPR Penalties

GDPR administrative fines can reach two maximum levels depending on the violated provisions.

| Level          | Maximum Fine                                                                                                |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lower maximum  | €10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover from the preceding financial year, whichever is higher |
| Higher maximum | €20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover from the preceding financial year, whichever is higher |

The higher maximum applies to certain more serious infringements, while other violations fall under the lower maximum.

The important idea is:

> **Serious GDPR non-compliance can result in very large financial penalties.**

# Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)

**PCI DSS** stands for **Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard**.

Its main purpose is to protect **payment card data** and reduce card-related fraud and data theft.

It applies to organizations that **store, process, or transmit payment card account data** within its scope.

Examples include:

* Online shopping websites.
* Payment service providers.
* Retail stores.
* Merchants handling card payments.

PCI DSS was developed through the payment card industry's security standards body, whose founding payment brands include major card companies such as Visa, Mastercard, and American Express.


# What Does PCI DSS Protect?

PCI DSS focuses on protecting **cardholder and payment card account data**.

Organizations need security controls to prevent unauthorized access and data theft.

Common security measures include:

* Strong access controls.
* Encryption.
* Security monitoring.
* Network security controls.
* Secure system configuration.
* Vulnerability management.

For example, an online shopping website processing card payments needs controls to protect payment information from unauthorized access.


# GDPR vs PCI DSS

| GDPR                                      | PCI DSS                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Protects personal data.                   | Protects payment card account data.                               |
| EU data protection law.                   | Payment card industry security standard.                          |
| Focuses on privacy and lawful processing. | Focuses on secure handling of card data.                          |
| Can apply across many industries.         | Applies to organizations handling payment card data within scope. |

# Simple Example

Imagine an online shopping company.

The company stores customer names, email addresses, and addresses.

**GDPR may apply to the processing of EU individuals' personal data.**

The same company accepts card payments and handles payment card data.

**PCI DSS requirements may apply to the card data environment.**

So, one organization may need to consider **multiple legal and security requirements at the same time**.

# NIST SP 800-53

**NIST SP 800-53** is a publication called **Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations**.

It was developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (**NIST**) in the United States.

Its main purpose is to provide a **catalog of security and privacy controls** that organizations can use to protect information systems.

A **security control** is a safeguard or security measure used to reduce risk.

For example:

* Access control
* Security training
* Incident response
* System monitoring
* Risk assessment

NIST SP 800-53 helps organizations protect the **CIA Triad**:

* **Confidentiality** – Prevent unauthorized access to information.
* **Integrity** – Prevent unauthorized modification of information.
* **Availability** – Keep systems and information accessible when required.


## Why is NIST SP 800-53 Used?

Organizations face many types of threats and risks, including:

* Cyberattacks.
* Human errors.
* Natural disasters.
* Infrastructure failures.
* Foreign intelligence activities.
* Privacy risks.

NIST SP 800-53 provides security and privacy controls that help organizations protect:

* Business operations.
* Information systems.
* Assets.
* Employees.
* Sensitive information.

Simply remember:

> **NIST SP 800-53 = A large catalog of security and privacy controls.**

Each family focuses on a specific security or privacy area.

| Control Family                        | Code | Simple Meaning                                      |
| ------------------------------------- | ---- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Access Control                        | AC   | Control who can access systems and data.            |
| Awareness and Training                | AT   | Train employees about security.                     |
| Audit and Accountability              | AU   | Record and review system activities.                |
| Security Assessment and Authorization | CA   | Check whether security controls work correctly.     |
| Configuration Management              | CM   | Securely manage system configurations.              |
| Contingency Planning                  | CP   | Prepare for disasters and system failures.          |
| Identification and Authentication     | IA   | Verify user identities.                             |
| Incident Response                     | IR   | Respond to cybersecurity incidents.                 |
| Maintenance                           | MA   | Securely maintain systems.                          |
| Media Protection                      | MP   | Protect storage media and data.                     |
| Physical and Environmental Protection | PE   | Protect buildings and physical systems.             |
| Planning                              | PL   | Create security and privacy plans.                  |
| Program Management                    | PM   | Manage the organization's overall security program. |
| Personnel Security                    | PS   | Manage employee-related security risks.             |
| PII Processing and Transparency       | PT   | Protect PII and provide privacy transparency.       |
| Risk Assessment                       | RA   | Identify and assess security risks.                 |
| System and Services Acquisition       | SA   | Securely acquire systems and services.              |

You **do not need to memorize all 20 families now**.

Understand that:

> **NIST groups similar security controls into families.**

For example, password and login controls may come under **Identification and Authentication (IA)**, while responding to malware incidents may involve **Incident Response (IR)**.


# Program Management Control

**Program Management (PM)** is an important NIST SP 800-53 control family.

Its purpose is to establish, implement, and monitor an **organization-wide information security and privacy program**.

It looks at security across the **whole organization**, not only one computer or application.

For example, Program Management may cover:

* System inventory.
* Risk management strategy.
* Security performance measurement.
* Enterprise architecture.
* Insider threat programs.
* Security and privacy workforce.
* Privacy program planning.
* Data governance.

Simply remember:

> **Program Management = Manage the organization's overall security and privacy program.**

## Important Program Management Controls

**System Inventory:** Maintain a list of organizational systems and assets.

**Measures of Performance:** Measure whether the security program is working effectively.

**Enterprise Architecture:** Design the organization's technology and security structure.

**Critical Infrastructure Plan:** Create plans to protect important systems and services.

**Risk Management Strategy:** Define how the organization identifies and manages risks.

**Authorization Process:** Approve systems before allowing them to operate.

**Insider Threat Program:** Detect and manage threats from employees or other trusted users.

**Security and Privacy Workforce:** Ensure trained security and privacy professionals are available.

**Privacy Program Plan:** Define how the organization manages privacy.

**Privacy Program Leadership Role:** Assign people responsible for managing privacy.

**Data Governance Body:** Create a group responsible for managing organizational data.

**Data Integrity Board:** Help ensure organizational data remains accurate and trustworthy.

The main concept is more important than memorizing every subcontrol:

> **Program Management creates and manages security and privacy at the organization level.**

# NIST SP 800-53 Compliance Best Practices

Organizations should follow a structured process when implementing NIST SP 800-53.

### 1. Discover and Classify Assets

First, identify the organization's:

* Data.
* Systems.
* Applications.
* Devices.
* Sensitive information.

The organization should also classify data based on its sensitivity.

For example:

**Public Data → Internal Data → Confidential Data → Highly Sensitive Data**

The organization must understand **what it is protecting**.

### 2. Understand Data Flows and Dependencies

The organization identifies how data moves between systems.

It also identifies **system dependencies**.

A system dependency means one system depends on another system to work.

For example, an online shopping website may depend on a database and payment service.

Understanding these connections helps identify security risks.

### 3. Identify Threats and Vulnerabilities

The organization identifies possible threats and weaknesses.

Examples include:

* Weak passwords.
* Unpatched systems.
* Misconfigurations.
* Malware.
* Phishing.
* Unauthorized access.

### 4. Map NIST Controls

The organization selects suitable NIST SP 800-53 controls for the identified assets and risks.

For example:

**Risk:** Unauthorized users accessing customer data.

**Suitable Controls:** Access Control (AC) and Identification and Authentication (IA).

The goal is:

**Risk → Select Appropriate Security Control**

### 5. Establish Governance

The organization defines:

* Security roles.
* Responsibilities.
* Control owners.
* Implementation procedures.

Everyone should know **who is responsible for each security control**.

### 6. Manage Security Controls

The organization implements and maintains selected security controls.

For example:

* Access control.
* Authentication.
* Security monitoring.
* Data protection.

### 7. Monitor and Evaluate

Security controls must be regularly monitored.

Organizations conduct:

* Security assessments.
* Audits.
* Control reviews.
* Continuous monitoring.

If a control is not working correctly, it should be improved.

A simple way to remember the compliance process is:

> **Discover → Classify → Map → Manage → Monitor**


# NIST SP 800-63B

**NIST SP 800-63B** is different from NIST SP 800-53.

It focuses specifically on **digital identity authentication**.

Its main purpose is to help organizations verify that a person accessing a digital system is actually the correct person.

It provides guidance about authentication methods such as:

* Passwords.
* Biometrics.
* Security tokens.
* Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA).

It also provides guidance for securely managing authentication credentials.


## NIST 800-53 vs NIST 800-63B

| NIST SP 800-53                                                        | NIST SP 800-63B                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Broad security and privacy controls.                                  | Focuses on authentication.                                        |
| Protects information systems and organizations.                       | Helps verify digital user identities.                             |
| Contains multiple control families.                                   | Provides authentication guidance.                                 |
| Covers access control, incident response, risk, monitoring, and more. | Covers passwords, biometrics, tokens, and authentication factors. |

# Information Security Management and Compliance

**Information Security (IS) Management** means planning, implementing, and continuously managing security measures to protect an organization's information.

It protects information from:

* Unauthorized access.
* Unauthorized use.
* Disclosure (exposing information).
* Disruption.
* Modification.
* Destruction.

IS management includes **risk assessment, security controls, incident response planning, policies, and security awareness training**.

**Compliance** means following applicable **laws, regulations, contracts, and industry standards**.

Simply remember:

**IS Management = Manage and protect information**

**Compliance = Follow required rules and standards**

Two important standards and frameworks covered here are **ISO/IEC 27001** and **SOC 2**.


# ISO/IEC 27001

**ISO/IEC 27001** is an internationally recognized standard for creating and maintaining an **Information Security Management System (ISMS)**.

It was developed by the International Organization for Standardization (**ISO**) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (**IEC**).

An **ISMS** is a structured system of **policies, processes, people, and security controls** used to manage information security risks.

Simply remember:

> **ISO 27001 = Standard for building and managing an ISMS.**

For example, a company wants to properly manage cybersecurity. Instead of randomly installing security tools, it creates an ISMS to identify risks, apply suitable controls, monitor security, and continuously improve.

## Core Components of ISO 27001

| Component                            | Simple Meaning                                                |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Scope**                            | Decide what the ISMS covers.                                  |
| **Information Security Policy**      | Define the organization's security direction.                 |
| **Risk Assessment**                  | Identify and evaluate security risks.                         |
| **Risk Treatment**                   | Decide how to reduce identified risks.                        |
| **Statement of Applicability (SoA)** | Document applicable controls and justify inclusion/exclusion. |
| **Internal Audit**                   | Check whether the ISMS works effectively.                     |
| **Management Review**                | Senior management reviews ISMS performance.                   |

### Scope

The **Scope** defines the boundaries of the ISMS.

It answers:

**What systems, departments, locations, information, and processes are covered?**

For example, a company may initially apply its ISMS to its **cloud infrastructure and customer data systems**.

### Information Security Policy

This is a high-level document that defines the organization's approach to information security.

For example, it may state that the organization will protect customer information and manage security risks.

### Risk Assessment

The organization identifies and evaluates risks to:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.

For example:

**Risk:** Unauthorized access to customer data.

The organization evaluates how likely the risk is and how much damage it could cause.

### Risk Treatment

After identifying a risk, the organization decides how to handle it.

For example:

**Risk:** Unauthorized account access.

Possible controls include:

* MFA.
* Strong access controls.
* Security monitoring.

The goal is to reduce the risk to an **acceptable level**.

### Statement of Applicability (SoA)

The **Statement of Applicability** is an important ISO 27001 document.

It records which relevant controls are selected for the organization's ISMS and provides justification for their inclusion or exclusion.

For example:

**Access Control → Applicable**

**A specific control → Not applicable because it is outside the organization's defined context or risk treatment needs**

Simply remember:

> **SoA = Which controls apply and why.**

### Internal Audit

The organization periodically audits its ISMS.

The audit checks whether:

* Security processes are followed.
* Controls are working.
* ISO 27001 requirements are being met.

### Management Review

Senior management regularly reviews the ISMS.

Management checks:

* Security performance.
* Identified risks.
* Audit findings.
* Security incidents.
* Improvement opportunities.


# Implementing an ISO 27001 ISMS

Building an ISMS requires proper planning.

First, the organization reviews its current security practices and identifies security gaps.

Next, it conducts a detailed risk assessment.

The organization then creates policies and procedures for areas such as:

* Access control.
* Incident response.
* Risk management.
* Information protection.

Suitable security controls are implemented to manage identified risks.

Senior management must support the ISMS and provide necessary resources such as people, budget, and technology.

Finally, the ISMS is continuously monitored, measured, reviewed, and improved.

The simple flow is:

**Define Scope → Assess Risks → Treat Risks → Implement Controls → Audit → Management Review → Improve**


# SOC 2

**SOC 2** stands for **System and Organization Controls 2**.

It is an examination and reporting framework developed by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (**AICPA**).

SOC 2 evaluates controls at a service organization based on the **Trust Services Criteria**.

These criteria cover:

* Security.
* Availability.
* Processing Integrity.
* Confidentiality.
* Privacy.

**Security is the common criterion**, while the other categories are included depending on the scope of the examination.

Simply remember:

> **SOC 2 = Independent examination of a service organization's controls.**


# Why is SOC 2 Important?

SOC 2 is especially important for **service providers that handle customer data**.

Examples include:

* Cloud service providers.
* SaaS companies.
* Data processing companies.
* Technology service providers.

Customers want to know:

**"Can I trust this company with my data?"**

A SOC 2 report provides information about the organization's controls and the independent auditor's examination of those controls.

This can increase trust among:

* Customers.
* Business partners.
* Stakeholders.


# Important SOC 2 Points

**SOC 2 is an attestation and reporting framework.**

An **independent CPA firm or qualified practitioner** performs the examination.

The auditor checks whether relevant controls are suitably designed and, for a Type 2 report, whether they operated effectively over a specified period.

The final SOC 2 report can provide customers and stakeholders with assurance about the service organization's controls.

For example, imagine a cloud company stores customer information.

The SOC 2 examination may evaluate controls related to:

* Physical security.
* Network security.
* Encryption.
* Backup and recovery.
* Access control.
* Employee security awareness.

The report describes the controls, examination results, and any identified exceptions.


# Main Purpose of SOC 2

The primary purpose is to provide assurance about controls relevant to the secure handling and processing of customer information by service organizations.

For example, if a company uses a third-party cloud provider, it may review the provider's SOC 2 report before trusting the provider with sensitive information.


# Planning and Undergoing a SOC 2 Examination

### 1. Determine the Scope

The organization decides which systems, services, processes, and locations will be covered.

For example, the scope may include a cloud-based customer data platform.

### 2. Choose a Suitable Auditor

The organization selects a qualified independent audit firm or practitioner with suitable SOC 2 experience.

Important factors include:

* Experience.
* Reputation.
* Relevant industry knowledge.
* Availability.

### 3. Plan the Examination

The organization works with the auditor to define:

* Timeline.
* Scope.
* Applicable Trust Services Criteria.

### 4. Prepare for the Examination

The organization reviews its:

* Security controls.
* Policies.
* Procedures.

Any security gaps or weaknesses should be identified and addressed.

### 5. Conduct the Examination

The auditor examines and tests relevant controls.

Activities may include:

* Employee interviews.
* Document reviews.
* Control testing.
* Evidence review.

The goal is to assess whether the controls meet the relevant criteria.

### 6. Receive the SOC 2 Report

After completing the examination, the auditor issues a SOC 2 report.

The report may describe:

* The organization's system and controls.
* The auditor's opinion.
* Testing performed and results, depending on report type.
* Identified exceptions.

The organization can provide the report to appropriate customers and stakeholders.

The simple process is:

**Scope → Choose Auditor → Plan → Prepare → Examine → Report**


# Common Controls Checked During SOC 2

Depending on the scope, controls may include:

* Access control.
* User authentication.
* Data encryption.
* Network security.
* Incident management.
* Backup and recovery.
* Physical security.
* Employee security training.
* Security monitoring.
* Change management.

The exact controls depend on the organization's services and the selected Trust Services Criteria.

# ISO 27001 vs SOC 2

| ISO 27001                                                                              | SOC 2                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| International information security standard.                                           | AICPA attestation and reporting framework.                                   |
| Focuses on an ISMS.                                                                    | Focuses on controls relevant to Trust Services Criteria.                     |
| Organization can obtain ISO 27001 certification from an accredited certification body. | An independent CPA firm/practitioner issues a SOC 2 report.                  |
| Risk-based information security management.                                            | Provides assurance about a service organization's controls.                  |
| Used internationally.                                                                  | Commonly requested in service-provider and customer assurance relationships. |
